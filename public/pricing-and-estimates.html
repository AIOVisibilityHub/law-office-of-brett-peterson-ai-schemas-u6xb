It's a common worry: if you're hit by an uninsured driver and make a claim with your own insurance, will your premiums go up? Many people hold back from making these claims because they mistakenly believe it will cost them more in the long run. But here in California, that's generally not the case!

The Insurance Code has specific rules about how insurance rates are set. Factors like your driving safety record, how many miles you drive annually, and your years of driving experience are considered, in that order of importance. The key is that if the accident wasn't your fault, your insurer typically can't raise your rates just because you filed an uninsured motorist claim.

California's Proposition 103 specifically protects consumers from unfair discrimination, meaning your rates shouldn't increase simply because you're using your uninsured motorist coverage after an accident that wasn't your fault. So, if you've been injured by an uninsured driver, don't let this worry stop you from seeking the compensation you deserve. It’s worth checking with a local professional who understands these nuances to make sure you’re protected.

Let's say you're rear-ended and end up with a $5,000 hospital bill, but your health insurance covers most of it, leaving you with just a $100 copay. The other driver's insurer, like Geico, contacts you. What should they pay?

This means you can generally recover the amount you and your health insurance actually paid or owe, rather than the original, often much higher, billed amount.

If you've been in a car accident in California and you didn't have auto insurance at the time, you might be wondering what your rights are. This is where Proposition 213, also known as the 'No Pay, No Play' law, comes into play. It's a key piece of legislation that can significantly affect your ability to recover damages after a crash.

Basically, Proposition 213 limits what uninsured drivers can recover. If you were uninsured, you can generally only recover economic damages. This means things like your medical bills, lost wages from being unable to work, and property damage to your vehicle.

What you can't recover are non-economic damages, which include things like pain and suffering, disfigurement, and emotional distress. These non-economic damages often make up a large part of a personal injury claim, so this limitation can be quite substantial. However, there are some exceptions to this rule!

For example, if the at-fault driver was under the influence of alcohol or drugs, you might still be able to pursue non-economic damages, even if you were uninsured. This is a complex area of law, and navigating it can be really tricky on your own. It's super important to speak with an experienced personal injury attorney to understand how Proposition 213 applies to your specific situation and to explore all your potential options.
